Backstepping Temporal Difference Learning

arXiv:2302.09875 · 2023

Han-Dong Lim, Donghwan Lee

Department of Electrical Engineering, KAIST, Daejeon, South Korea

Paper (PDF) arXiv Conference Video BibTeX

Design stable continuous-time dynamics first, then recover an off-policy temporal-difference learning algorithm.

Key Contributions

Reverse the usual design sequence

Instead of first choosing an optimization objective, the framework constructs stable ODE dynamics and then derives sampled TD updates whose mean behavior follows those dynamics.

A new single-time-scale family

Backstepping TD introduces a design parameter and coupled auxiliary/value updates sharing one step-size sequence. Setting that parameter to zero recovers GTD2 within the proposed family.

Connect existing correction methods

Related backstepping constructions recover or generalize single-time-scale TDC and TDC++ variants, linking multiple off-policy algorithms through stabilizing feedback design rather than only objective functions.

Abstract

A summary of the paper, written for this page.

Combining off-policy sampling, bootstrapping, and function approximation can make ordinary TD learning diverge. This paper develops a control-theoretic route to stable algorithms in the linear-approximation setting. It starts with continuous-time dynamics linked to the projected Bellman equation, uses backstepping to construct stabilizing feedback, and converts those dynamics into implementable stochastic updates. The resulting backstepping TD algorithm uses a single time scale and includes GTD2 as a special case. The paper proves asymptotic convergence under stochastic-approximation assumptions, relates the framework to correction-based TD methods, and evaluates behavior on standard policy-evaluation benchmarks.

The problem

Stability under off-policy evaluation

Policy evaluation may use data collected by a behavior policy different from the target policy. Importance weighting addresses that mismatch, but ordinary TD with linear features can have unstable mean dynamics. The problem is to design updates that retain the projected Bellman fixed point while giving those dynamics a stability certificate and remaining implementable from sampled transitions and feature vectors.

From problem to solution

How the method works

Create a virtual stabilizing input

Represent the parameter error and an auxiliary variable as interconnected continuous-time states. Choose a virtual feedback that cancels the problematic coupling in the first subsystem, leaving a stable feature-covariance term.

Section 3.1 and Lemma 3.1, p. 6

Backstep and sample the dynamics

Introduce the error between the actual state and the virtual input, build a composite Lyapunov function, and choose feedback for the full system. Translate the closed-loop equations into sampled updates and apply the ODE stochastic-approximation theorem.

Section 3.1; Appendix 7.4.1, pp. 7, 14–15

Vc(λ,z) = ½‖λ‖2 + ½‖z‖2,   z = x − ηλ

Section 3.1 and Appendix 7.4.1: the composite Lyapunov function after the backstepping coordinate change. Here x is value-parameter error, λ is the auxiliary state, and η is the design parameter. Its derivative is nonpositive; the proof then invokes LaSalle's invariance principle.

Main results

What the analysis establishes

Almost-sure asymptotic convergence

Theorem 3.1 states convergence of BTD's value parameter to the projected Bellman fixed point with probability one under the Robbins–Monro step-size condition and the paper's stochastic-approximation setup. It is not a finite-time error-rate theorem.

Theorem 3.1; Assumption 7.1, pp. 7, 12

A recognized algorithm is recovered

Remark 3.1 identifies GTD2 at the zero design-parameter setting. Other constructions yield convergence statements for correction-based variants, but their parameter restrictions differ and must be checked separately.

Remark 3.1 and Sections 3.2–3.3, pp. 7–9

Empirical sensitivity to the parameter

Experiments use Baird's counterexample, random-walk variants, and the Boyan chain, measuring projected Bellman error. Performance changes with the design parameter; the reported setting favored on several tasks is not best on Baird's example.

Section 4, Table 1, p. 9

Reading the guarantees

Assumptions and scope

The theoretical setting uses finite MDPs, linear features with full column rank, well-defined importance weights, and a well-posed projected Bellman fixed point. The probability-one convergence argument relies on the stated stochastic-approximation assumptions and diminishing Robbins–Monro step sizes. Constant-step experiments illustrate practical behavior but do not themselves establish that asymptotic theorem. A stable continuous-time construction does not imply stability for every finite discrete step size. Nonlinear value approximation is left for future work, and no single parameter setting is shown to dominate all benchmarks.

See the reviewed paper version for complete hypotheses, constants, and proofs.
