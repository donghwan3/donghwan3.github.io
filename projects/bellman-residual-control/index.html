Bellman Residual Minimization for Control: Geometry, Stationarity, and Convergence

arXiv:2601.18840 · 2026

Donghwan Lee, Hyukjun Yang

School of Electrical Engineering, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), Daejeon, South Korea

Paper (PDF) arXiv Conference Video BibTeX

The geometry of control Bellman residuals explains which stationary points descent methods can reach.

Key Contributions

Geometry of the control objective

The hard-max objective is continuous and piecewise quadratic over policy regions. Its generalized gradient handles ties between greedy actions instead of assuming that one policy remains active.

A projected-equation interpretation

Under a stated nonsingularity condition, stationarity implies an oblique projected control Bellman equation. This characterizes approximate solutions without requiring the projected Bellman map to be contractive.

Separate approximation and tabular guarantees

The analysis distinguishes convergence to stationary points under linear approximation from convergence to the unique Bellman solution in the tabular case. A smooth soft-control objective permits stronger rate guarantees.

Abstract

A summary of the paper, written for this page.

Bellman residual minimization offers an optimization-based route to control, but the action maximum makes its objective nonconvex and nonsmooth. This paper characterizes that geometry, derives the Clarke subdifferential, and connects stationary points to oblique projected Bellman equations. It analyzes minimum-norm generalized gradient descent and a differentiable soft variant. With linear approximation, the guarantees concern stationarity rather than global optimality. In the tabular setting, the unique stationary point is the appropriate Bellman fixed point, enabling stronger convergence statements and a geometric objective-decay result for the smooth formulation.

The problem

Can a nonconvex residual still provide useful control guarantees?

Squaring a Bellman residual looks like ordinary least squares, but optimizing actions changes the landscape whenever the greedy policy switches. With approximate value functions, a stationary point need not represent the globally optimal policy. The paper asks what these points mean, which descent rules are justified at nonsmooth boundaries, and when the stronger tabular structure recovers the exact Bellman solution.

From problem to solution

How the method works

Analyze policy regions and generalized gradients

Partition the parameter space by greedy policies and derive the Clarke subdifferential. For nonsmooth descent, select the minimum-norm generalized gradient and use Armijo backtracking; an arbitrary subgradient is not automatically a descent direction.

Sections 4.1-4.2, Theorems 1-3, pp. 4-6

Smooth the control maximum

Replace the maximum with a log-sum-exp soft operator. Derive an ordinary gradient and analyze its local Lipschitz constant on an iterate-containing compact set; step-size restrictions depend on this set.

Main results

What the analysis establishes

Stationarity under linear approximation

Every limit point of the specified nonsmooth method is Clarke stationary. The smooth method drives the gradient norm to zero under its step-size condition. Neither statement guarantees a globally optimal approximate policy.

Theorems 3 and 8, pp. 6 and 8

Exact solutions in the tabular case

The hard objective has the unique stationary point Q*, and minimum-norm generalized gradient descent with Armijo backtracking converges to it. The soft objective similarly has a unique stationary point at its own soft Bellman solution.

Theorems 4-5 and 9, pp. 6 and 8

A conditional geometric objective bound

The soft tabular objective satisfies a Polyak-Lojasiewicz inequality on compact sets. Together with the stated smoothness and step-size conditions, this yields geometric decay of objective error; the target is the soft fixed point.

Lemma 1 and Theorem 10, p. 9

Reading the guarantees

Assumptions and scope

The guarantees use exact Bellman residuals and finite discounted MDPs; they are not a model-free sample-complexity theorem. Linear features must have full column rank, and oblique-projection claims require the specified matrices to be nonsingular. Approximate stationary points may be nonunique. The nonsmooth convergence theorem uses a particular minimum-norm direction and line search. Smooth convergence requires a suitable step size, and its solution belongs to the soft control problem rather than automatically equaling the ordinary optimal Q-function.
