Suppressing Overestimation in Q-Learning through Adversarial Behaviors

arXiv:2310.06286 · 2023

HyeAnn Lee, Donghwan Lee

Department of Electrical Engineering, KAIST, Daejeon, South Korea

Paper (PDF) arXiv Conference BibTeX

A dummy adversary turns multiple Q-estimators into a framework for regulating maximization bias.

Key Contributions

A game interpretation of estimators

A dummy opponent chooses an estimator index and affects a reward shift while leaving transition probabilities unchanged. This connects bias-control updates with an existing Markov-game learning framework.

Two operator orderings

Maxmin and minmax DAQ reverse the order of action maximization and estimator minimization. Zero reward shifts recover their corresponding unshifted algorithms, allowing comparison within one construction.

Theory alongside benchmark evidence

The paper applies a switching-system convergence analysis to tabular DAQ and separately tests learning behavior on grid-world, Sutton, and Weng MDP examples with different reward structures.

Abstract

A summary of the paper, written for this page.

Maximizing noisy value estimates can make Q-learning overly optimistic. This paper introduces dummy adversarial Q-learning, which maintains multiple estimators and adds estimator-dependent reward shifts. The estimator index can be interpreted as the action of an artificial opponent in a zero-sum game. Because that opponent does not change the environment's transitions, the construction provides an alternative learning mechanism for the original decision problem. The framework includes maxmin and minmax variants, supports a finite-time tabular analysis, and is evaluated on small benchmark MDPs that expose the effects of overestimation and underestimation.

The problem

Controlling optimism without changing the task

The maximum over uncertain action values can favor actions whose estimates are accidentally high. Taking minima across estimators can counter this effect, but too much pessimism can also hinder learning. The challenge is to expose useful design choices for that balance while preserving the decision problem, and to distinguish a convergence guarantee for the learning estimates from environment-specific empirical benefits.

From problem to solution

How the method works

Shift rewards across estimators

Maintain several Q estimates and add an estimator-specific constant to the observed reward. Update using either a max-over-actions/min-over-estimators target or the reversed order. Synchronous and asynchronous implementations trade per-step computation against estimator update frequency.

Sections IV.A–IV.C, pp. 3–4

Interpret the update as a game

Treat the estimator index as a dummy adversary's action. The adversary affects the artificial reward component, not the transition law. This permits adapting finite-time minimax Q-learning analysis while interpreting the two operator orders as different turn orders.

Main results

What the analysis establishes

Finite-time expected-error control

Theorem 1 bounds asynchronous DAQ's expected sup-norm error relative to its corresponding shifted optimum. With constant step size, the bound contains decaying transient terms and a residual proportional to the square root of that step size.

Theorem 1, p. 5

Benchmark improvements are conditional

The reported grid-world and maximization-bias experiments show improved learning with selected DAQ configurations. Outcomes depend on reward shifts and environment structure; the comparisons also illustrate cases where optimism or pessimism changes which algorithm learns faster.

Section V.B, Figures 4–6, pp. 6–7

The theorem and experiments differ

The theoretical setup uses independent samples and fixed sampling distributions with full coverage. Experiments use epsilon-greedy behavior, and some episodic examples use an undiscounted setting. These experiments illustrate behavior beyond the stated theorem's assumptions.

Sections IV.E and V, pp. 4–7

Reading the guarantees

Assumptions and scope

The finite-time result covers finite tabular spaces with the stated boundedness, sampling, and constant-step assumptions; it does not certify general neural implementations. Reward shifts and the number of estimators are design choices whose useful settings depend on the environment. A fixed step size leaves a residual bound, so the theorem is not exact convergence at every fixed step size. The benchmark results do not establish uniform superiority. Deep Q-learning and actor-critic extensions are identified as future directions, not demonstrated guarantees.

See the reviewed paper version for complete hypotheses, constants, and proofs.
