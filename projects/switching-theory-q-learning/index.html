Switching Theory for Q-Learning

arXiv:2604.19569 · 2026

Donghwan Lee

Department of Electrical Engineering, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), Daejeon, South Korea

Paper (PDF) arXiv Video BibTeX

An exact switching-system representation exposes instance-dependent convergence rates of tabular Q-learning.

Key Contributions

An exact error representation

A statewise convexity argument replaces the Bellman-max difference with an average under a suitable stochastic policy. This removes the affine error offset while retaining the martingale noise.

A computable spectral rate

The joint spectral radius equals the largest ordinary spectral radius among deterministic-policy modes. An equivalent linear-programming characterization avoids explicitly enumerating all deterministic policies.

Sampling-aware finite-time bounds

Separate analyses cover independent samples and a single geometrically mixing Markov chain. Both retain the drift-controlled transients and the additional terms generated by sampling noise.

Abstract

A summary of the paper, written for this page.

This work rewrites the Q-learning error as a stochastic linear switching system without the affine offset used in earlier comparison analyses. Its deterministic drift is governed by a family of policy-dependent nonnegative matrices. The joint spectral radius of that family supplies an instance-dependent exponential rate, while stochastic bounds separately account for sampling noise. Special structure makes the rate computable through linear-programming feasibility and bisection. The paper treats independent observations and geometrically mixing Markovian observations, and illustrates why sharper drift rates need not mean uniformly better finite-time bounds.

The problem

Which rate belongs to the learning dynamics themselves?

A convenient row-sum estimate can obscure the actual worst-case growth of the matrices underlying Q-learning. Earlier switching formulations also introduce an affine offset that requires auxiliary comparisons. This paper asks whether the original error drift can be represented directly, whether its intrinsic matrix-family rate can be characterized, and how that rate can enter finite-time estimates without ignoring noise or transient constants.

From problem to solution

How the method works

Remove the affine offset

Express the difference between two action maxima as a convex combination of actionwise errors. The resulting predictable stochastic-policy matrix lies in the convex hull of the deterministic-policy family, giving an exact stochastic recursion.

Section 4, Theorem 1, p. 5

Compute a common contraction certificate

Exploit nonnegative policy structure to characterize the joint spectral radius. For a trial rate, test linear inequalities in state-action and state weights; bisection then approaches the rate while producing a weighted-norm certificate.

The error follows a predictable policy-dependent linear map plus sampling noise. In the independent-sampling setting, the noise is a martingale difference; the Markovian extension requires a separate decomposition.

Main results

What the analysis establishes

Independent-sampling error bound

Under full coverage and a constant step size in the stated range, expected error has joint-spectral-radius transients plus a residual upper bound. For fixed problem data, this residual term decreases with the square root of the step size.

Section 5, Theorem 2, p. 6

A Markovian extension

For an irreducible, aperiodic behavior-induced chain satisfying geometric mixing, a Poisson-equation argument controls temporal dependence. The same drift rate remains, with an additional mixing-time-dependent noise term.

Section 7, Theorem 5, pp. 7-8

Sharper rates on a constructed example

A small finite MDP has a joint spectral radius strictly below the row-sum bound. Simulation illustrates this distinction; the plotted normalized rate curves are not empirical sample-complexity bounds or a claim of universal practical superiority.

Section 8 and Figure 1, pp. 8-9

Reading the guarantees

Assumptions and scope

The results apply to finite discounted tabular Q-learning with positive sampling frequencies and fixed step sizes. The Markovian extension needs explicit mixing assumptions. A smaller exponential base alone does not establish a tighter bound at every iteration: product-growth constants, polynomial transient factors, and residual upper bounds also matter. The spectral rate is exact for the associated switching family, not a promise that every Q-learning trajectory attains its worst-case rate. Large linear programs may still be computationally demanding.
